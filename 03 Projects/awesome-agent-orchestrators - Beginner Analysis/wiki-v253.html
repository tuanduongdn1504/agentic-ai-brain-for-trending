I

The finding

On 29 July 2026 the maintainer opened a commit message with the sharpest sentence in the repository:

The list had grown past the point where a reader could compare entries. Descriptions ranged from bare taglines to 90-word feature dumps, and length tracked how much each submitter promoted their own project rather than what the tool does. commit aeecb0d — “Rewrite entry descriptions, add a routing guide, fix stale links (#121)”

In a self-submitted directory, description length measures promotional effort, not information. He rewrote 127 descriptions to a stated budget of “~15–25 words”, leading with mechanism. It worked: the longest entry in the file went from 74 words to 31, entries over 45 words went from six to zero, and in-band compliance went from 26% to 60%.

His arithmetic checks out, too. I verified the commit message against its own diff: “add 11 entries” is exactly 11; “fix 15 stale repo URLs” is exactly 15 (12 owner/repo migrations plus three case-only fixes); all five named rebrands are real; and the one entry he dropped as a duplicate is why the file has zero duplicates today.

Then, twenty-two days later, two populations coexist in the same file.

Why the fix could not hold

The editorial standard exists in exactly one place: the body of one commit message. There is no contributing.md — there never has been, across 188 commits — and nothing measures a word count. A submitter opening a pull request sees 180 entries ranging from 4 to 72 words and no stated budget at all.

A normalisation you perform by hand and record only in a commit message is an event, not a standard. Put the budget where the contributor writes, or measure it. the borrowable rule

This is the precise inverse of the previous entry in this corpus. There, a working measurement instrument shipped and the reading was deleted. Here the reading is impeccable — his own diagnosis, his own arithmetic, all correct — and the instrument was never built.

The linter would fail this file 21 times and be wrong 21 times

I fetched the official awesome-list requirements and tested the mechanical rules:

  • 17 entries do not end with a period. All 17 are exactly the 17 Resting entries — and in 17 of 17 the sentence is correctly punctuated, then followed by the italic decay tag _(last commit 2026-03)_. The violation is the decay policy.
  • 4 descriptions start lowercase. All four begin tmux or macOS — proper nouns that must stay lowercase. Capitalising them would introduce an error.

Zero of the 21 are defects. The rules that are mechanisable here are the merely stylistic ones; the rules that matter — is this entry true, is it proportionate, does it satisfy the admission test — are the ones no linter can check.

Meanwhile it fails the requirements that carry consequences. Quoting what I fetched: “Place a file named license or LICENSE in the repo root” — absent, never existed. “Has contribution guidelines… named contributing.md” — absent. And the list is not on the official Awesome list; I fetched and searched it. The one requirement it satisfies is the badge in its own first line.

III

What is genuinely well built

An admission test that is actually decidable

Line 5 states a four-way functional test: a tool qualifies if it decides what an agent works on, when it runs, where it runs, or what happens to its output — and takes whatever task you point it at. Excluded by name: “memory backends, MCP servers, sandbox providers, skill libraries.”

It resolves hard cases. Sandbox providers are out, but “where it runs” is in — so E2B and Daytona are excluded, while a tool that drives agents into them, or places them in Kubernetes Pods, is included. That distinction is not obvious, and the test makes it decidable from a candidate’s own README. I stress-tested it on the eight entries most likely to strain it; one clearly fails (a skill lockfile manager, against a named “skill libraries” exclusion) and one is borderline. Two boundary problems in 180 entries.

A dormant shelf that keeps its evidence

The last section is titled Resting: “A watchlist of projects without a push in the last few months (checked 2026-07-28). They stay here until they’re active again, then move back up.” Seventeen entries, each carrying its own per-entry evidence. Flag, never delete; evidence, not doubt.

And the judgements are sound. I fetched them: the lettabot claim is exact on all three components — archived on 26 May 2026, replaced by Letta Code. More telling, the list rested vibe-kanban at 27,900 stars, a project twenty times more popular than the list itself. Of the nine entries surviving from the very first commit, four have already been moved to Resting, and I found zero rot among the oldest still-active ones.

The self-listing is the most modest entry in the file

One entry in 180 belongs to the maintainer. There is no disclosure anywhere — I grepped for it and got zero hits, which is a real gap. And the entry is 10 words: “Minimal TUI for spawning parallel coding agents in git worktrees.” No feature list, no harness list, no licence brag. Across six revisions in seven months it never left the 10-word class, and in the normalisation commit he added the word “Minimal” to his own entry while cutting 126 others. Both halves belong in the record: the disclosure is missing, and the abuse is not there.

V

The defects, in severity order

A dead project sits in the active list — and fixing its link is what hid it

Coworker (10,900 stars, one commit) is listed as active. Its own README states verbatim: “This project is no longer supported.” It arrived in the 29 July “fix stale links” commit as one of the five named rebrands.

The mechanism: the Resting policy keys on push recency, and a rename resets that clock. A renamed repository looks maximally fresh and is maximally dead at the same time — so correcting the stale URL is precisely what concealed the staleness.

Licence disclosure is 4 of 180, and adversely selected

Three entries advertise MIT; one honourably declares BUSL-1.1, a disadvantage. But the two restrictive licences I happened to check are both silent and both high-profile: coder/mux is AGPL-3.0 and superset-sh/superset is Elastic 2.0. Small projects advertise permissive terms as a selling point; larger projects with copyleft or source-available terms say nothing. You cannot filter this list by licence — and for a commercial product that is the single most consequential gap.

The decay policy decayed

The checked YYYY-MM-DD stamp has had exactly two values in its life — 21 July and 28 July. Refreshed once, seven days apart, then never again across the 33 commits since. Today it is 23 days old, and nothing detects a Resting project that has become active again, which the policy explicitly promises to do.

Also: no licence file at all, so the default is all-rights-reserved and nobody can cleanly fork 180 curated judgements; and a bus factor of one, with 72 of 78 identities holding a single commit and 35 pull requests open against 100 merged.

One false finding I nearly filed

I almost recorded humanlayer as a policy inconsistency: its newest commit is 19 June, two months before today, while a project last touched in April sits in Resting. But the stamp says checked 28 July — and on that date the gap was about five weeks, which is correctly not “a few months”. The policy was applied correctly; only the stamp has aged. Evaluate a dated claim against its own date, not against today.

VIII

The verdict

Goal-aligned include, 3 of 4 — and no mint

Criterion (a) fails: the profile states no company, no location, and no Anthropic affiliation, and inference from a name or from notability is forbidden. (b) is cleanly strong: this is the definitive index of the one tool category with twenty ships and zero pilots behind it. (c) is strong but tempered by the defects above. (d) is strong — it converts a long-open decision into a ranked shortlist in an afternoon.

No mint. Counts unchanged. This is a clean instance-strengthening of a genre pattern confirmed 222 ships ago, on five grounds: the pattern already covers it; a curated list scoped to one domain is domain-not-capability; the novel part is an editorial technique; it is not world-first and its precedence rests on a mocked API, so it is not established; and the anti-re-accumulation discipline stands at 51 live standalones against a 1.4k-star, 233-line anchor.

The strongest alternative is recorded rather than executed: a standalone for a curated directory with a declared inclusion criterion and a dated decay section — genuinely distinctive, because of the genre’s prior instances none pairs an admission procedure with a retirement procedure. Most awesome lists have a topic and an alphabet. This one has both gates. It still loses on the five grounds above.

What to actually do

  • Take the admission test as a mint gate. Four ships’ worth of consistent capability-versus-technique-versus-domain rulings become one citable four-way test.
  • Take the Resting section for the stale-row backlog that has been deferred roughly 65 ships. It has never happened because deletion demands certainty; moving a row is reversible, so it can happen this week.
  • Then write the verification script that four consecutive ships have now specified and nobody has written — with two new clauses this entry supplies: a byte budget on the head blocks that have been hand-compacted three times, and a freshness check on every dated “checked” notice in the vault. Because the lesson here is not that the maintainer was careless. It is that he wrote an excellent standard in a place nothing reads, and 22 days later the numbers proved it.
