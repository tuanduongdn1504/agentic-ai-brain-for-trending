## Five-Step Email Writing Methodology

The core framework for writing high-performing emails rests on five sequential steps, each with a distinct purpose:

### Step 1: Define the Email Goal

- Email copy succeeds only when it maintains **one unified objective**—clarity on what the message exists to accomplish.
- Before writing, state explicitly: *What is this email designed to do?*
- A clear goal ensures copy stays focused and doesn't scatter across multiple messages or contradicting signals.
- Examples: "Move subscriber to click the link," "Build trust before the pitch," "Handle a specific micro-objection."

### Step 2: Choose Your Deployment Method (Choose Your Angle)

- Select from proven email templates/structures rather than inventing from blank page: **"Model, do not create."**
- Choose an angle that matches your specific goal, audience awareness stage, and core belief you want to install.
- Common proven methods: **[[core-email-templates-pas-bab-educational|PAS (Problem-Agitate-Solution)]], [[core-email-templates-pas-bab-educational|BAB (Before-After-Bridge)]], [[storytelling-golden-structure-6-point|Storytelling]], Educational (belief-breaking), or [[hooks-curiosity-loops-five-formulas|Hook + Value first]]**.
- This "skeleton" prevents rambling and ensures structural integrity before materials are added.

### Step 3: Research + Gather Materials (80% of Real Work Happens Here)

- Research is **not optional**—it's the foundational phase where email quality is actually determined.
- Extract materials directly relevant to your core belief/goal:
  - **Pain points & desires** of your target 20% segment (deep, hyper-specific ones, not generic).
  - **Logic chains** (why old methods fail, why new approach works).
  - **Stories** (customer transformations, failures-then-success arcs, relatable struggles).
  - **Testimonials** (especially specific outcomes with numbers).
  - **Micro-objections** (small doubts you can address inside the email itself).
  - **Product features** that directly solve the pain you've identified (ignore irrelevant features).
- **Pre-process** raw materials if they're too abstract: Convert logic into analogies, name frameworks, make vague stories more vivid.
- Example: If your raw insight is "service owners hit revenue ceiling," pre-process it into "Capacity Selling Trap: When you sell time directly, you trade hours for dollars, capping revenue at ~$X. You need engines (systems) not more hours."
- Goal of this step: Enter Step 4 with a **complete, organized kit of materials** ready to assemble—no scrambling mid-draft.

### Step 4: Draft Without Self-Editing

- Assemble materials into the skeleton template you chose in Step 2.
- Write in the **customer's voice/tone** (use their vocabulary, pacing, rhythm).
- Do **not** interrupt flow to edit, rewrite, or critique.
- First draft will be rough—this is expected and correct.
- Focus: **Maintain structure integrity + match voice guide + fill skeleton** completely from top to bottom.
- Example process: "Here's the hook template. Insert materials here. Body section now. CTA + urgency layer."
- Resistance to perfectionism here is critical—rough draft with right structure > polished draft with weak structure.

### Step 5: Edit (80% of the Time Here)

- **This is where 80% of email quality is actually made.**
- Edit is not optional polishing—it's the core value-creation phase.
- Refine systematically:
  - **Wording**: Replace vague language with specific language. "Khó khăn" (difficulties) → specify the exact difficulty.
  - **Logic flow**: Verify each sentence builds on prior one; catch logic gaps.
  - **Specificity**: Replace abstract claims with numbers, timeframes, examples. "Tiết kiệm được thời gian" (saves time) → "saves 2.5 hours per week = 130 hours/year = 3 full weeks of extra free time."
  - **Redundancy**: Cut duplicate points, tangents, unnecessary phrases.
  - **Comprehension**: Simplify sentences; use shorter paragraphs to lower reading friction.
  - **Emotional resonance**: Ensure feeling/intent comes through; add specific sensory details or emotion-words where absent.
  - **Completeness**: Check for typos, missing links, broken arguments.
- Final pass: Read end-to-end as reader, not author. Does every line pull weight? Does the argument hold? Does it move?

## Email Architecture: 3-Question Planning Framework

Before writing a single word, create an **email architecture**—a simple blueprint answering three questions:

### Question 1: Who + What Awareness Stage?

- **Who** is the core 20% segment this email targets? (Hyper-specific, not "entrepreneurs.")
- **What awareness stage** are they in?
  - Problem-aware: Know they have pain, not yet solution-conscious.
  - Solution-aware: Know solution exists, unsure if *this* solution fits them.
  - Product-aware: Know your product, still skeptical on efficacy/value.
  - Objection stage: Almost convinced, but have specific doubts.
- **Example**: *"Freelance copywriters who've been told cold email is dead + are now solution-aware that coaching/training exists but haven't tried it yet; they're stuck on pricing objections."*
- This clarifies your **opening tone**. Don't re-explain the pain to solution-aware readers; go straight to handling their specific skepticism.

### Question 2: Where Should I Lead Them?

- What's the **specific next step** you want them to take?
- Not vague ("Learn more") but concrete ("Click to watch 3-min demo" or "Schedule call").
- This end-point shapes everything before it.
- **Example**: *"They should click the link to see case study showing this method generates 3× conversion vs their current email."*

### Question 3: What ONE Core Belief Do I Want Installed?

- Not multiple messages—**one belief you're either introducing or reinforcing**.
- This is the **through-line** that prevents the email from becoming a rambling mess.
- The entire middle section serves this single belief.
- **Example**: *"Belief: Copywriting success depends on research quality (80% work), not wordcraft (20% work). This changes their strategy from 'write more emails' to 'spend time researching first.'"*
- Alternative example: *"Belief: The dark side of freelance copywriting is burnout from oscillating workload; but the tradeoff for freedom is worth it IF you accept the intensity."*

## Research Prioritization: What to Dig For

The course teaches a deliberate hierarchy for what materials to hunt down:

### Start with Customer Pain (20% Deep)

- Don't list generic problems; identify the **sharpest pain point** unique to your 20% segment.
- Go beyond surface: If pain is "low income," dig deeper—is it shame calling parents for money? Fear of graduation? Exhaustion from part-time work?
- Vividness in pain = higher conversion later.
- Example from transcript: Instead of generic "students need money," go to: *"11 PM in 12m² dorm room in Saigon, eating 15,000đ rice, AC at 35°C. End of month, I call home: 'Mom, can you send 2 million đ?' Shame floods in."* ← Specific, visceral, relatable.

### Then Logic + Proofs

- Once you've nailed the pain, extract **why the old approach fails**.
- Find testimonials, case studies, data, counterexamples that show new method works.
- Prioritize **social proof** (what similar people achieved) over abstract claims.

### Gather Micro-Objections + Preemptive Answers

- As you research, note small doubts that emerge: "This takes forever to learn," "I don't have time," "What if I'm not good at writing?"
- Pre-answer these inside the email so they don't block action.

## Deliberate Practice Framework (Compress Learning 2-3×)

The course teaches a copy-specific adaptation of Benjamin Franklin's learning method:

### The Framework: Study → Extract → Rewrite → Compare → Identify Weakness → Improve

- **Study**: Handwrite 1-2 great email samples daily (handwriting embeds structure deeper than reading).
- **Extract**: Ask "What is the one key idea or mechanism that makes this email work?"
- **Rewrite**: Rewrite the email from scratch without referencing the original; see if you can regenerate the key idea.
- **Compare**: Line up your version vs. the original. Where did you diverge? Where did you simplify or complicate?
- **Identify weakness**: Ask "What did I miss? What makes the original version better?" Name the specific failure mode.
- **Improve**: Rewrite again, fixing that specific weakness.

### Why It Works

- **Not 'practice makes perfect'** but **deliberate makes perfect.** Random writing time ≠ learning.
- This 6-step loop, repeated on 2-3 samples weekly, compresses learning 2-3× faster than amorphous practice.
- Captures not just "what works" but "why it works and how to regenerate it."
- Keeps feedback tight (you're comparing against known-good models).

### Discipline Notes

- Handwriting is load-bearing—typewritten copying skips the kinesthetic embedding.
- Pick **diverse models** (PAS email, storytelling email, educational email) to build transferable patterns, not rote memorization.
- Don't just copy grammar—extract the **reasoning** (e.g., "This hook works because it breaks reader expectation by claiming the opposite of what I assumed").

## The 80/20 Editing Principle

The course emphasizes this counterintuitive truth repeatedly:

- **80% of copy quality lives in editing, not writing.**
- The first draft is _supposed_ to be rough; roughness is not a sign of failure.
- Professional copywriters often spend 20% of time assembling the draft and **80% refinement**—removing clutter, strengthening logic, adding specificity, tuning tone.
- Common beginner mistake: Spending equal time on draft and edit, or worse, treating a rough draft as "done."
- **Shift your mindset**: The draft is raw material. Editing is where the real skill shows.

### What Gets Cut in Editing

- Redundant points (same idea expressed twice in different words).
- Vague language ("khó khăn" → specify the exact struggle).
- Statements without proof (claims need logic or evidence).
- Sections that digress from the one core belief.
- Weak word choices (passive voice, generic adjectives).

### What Gets Added in Editing

- Specific numbers, timeframes, contextual anchors.
- Proof/evidence where assertions appear.
- Analogies or metaphors to clarify abstract concepts.
- Emotional anchors (words conveying feeling, not just information).
- Transitions to tighten logic flow.

## Why Research Is 80%, Writing Is 20%

The course frames the writing workload as:

- **80% research** = Understanding target segment deeply, identifying pain, finding proof, gathering stories, pre-processing materials.
- **20% writing** = Arranging materials into template, drafting from skeleton, refining language.

**Rationale**: An average writer with excellent materials beats an excellent writer with poor materials. Research is the bottleneck. Once you have rich materials + clear structure, the writing is mostly mechanical assembly. Most copywriters fail because they skip research and try to write from intuition.

## Key Takeaways

- **Five-step process is load-bearing**: Define goal → Choose method → Research (materials kit) → Draft (assemble freely) → Edit (80% time here). Skip any step and copy suffers.

- **Email architecture** (three questions: who + stage, where to lead, one core belief) prevents scope-creep and keeps copy single-threaded. Spend 10 minutes on this before Step 3.

- **Research is not optional; it's 80% of the work.** Vague materials lead to vague copy. Deep pain points, specific proofs, and pre-processed logic are what actually persuade.

- **Edit is not optional finishing; it's the quality creation phase.** Allocate 80% of your copywriting time here—to removing redundancy, adding specificity, tuning emotion, verifying logic.

- **Deliberate practice (study → extract → rewrite → compare → identify → improve) compresses learning 2-3×.** Not random writing practice, but structured iteration against known-good models builds transferable skill faster.

- **"Model, do not create"**: In early stages, choose proven templates/angles rather than inventing. Mastery comes later; initially, follow the skeleton and fill it well.
